## 正文

作者月费 $70 买三个编码 Agent，而不是 $100 买一个更深的池：

- Claude Pro + Claude Code：$20
- ChatGPT Plus + Codex：$20
- SuperGrok + Grok Build：$30

每个订阅有自己的用量池。一个撞周限或短期限，另外两个还能用。一个 Agent 反复走死胡同，就把仓库交给另一套假设的系统。

当前分工（会变）：Claude 做难题/架构/调试；Codex 做长战役、迁移、测到绿；Grok Build 做绿地和 UI，也当第三编码池。

交接才是系统。三个终端贴同一份巨型提示会浪费额度、互相改。到边界就停：跑相关测试、提交检查点，给下一个 Agent 一份短 brief：仓库/分支/commit、目标与完成定义、已完成、已试过且失败的原因、当前测试结果、不许动的文件、下一步、如何证明完成。尤其是「已经试过」——没有这一行，接收方会用自己的额度再发现同一条死路。下一个 Agent 从仓库和 diff 重建理解，旧 transcript 通常留下。同一工作树只留一个写者。

什么时候该上一档大套餐：只有一个供应商被批准；工作流深度绑死一个生态；反复耗尽那一家而备胎接不住；重建上下文比交接更贵；某个问题真的需要那一家的更多容量。

作者的瓶颈是覆盖面：需要在一家撞墙或不再推进时把任务挪走。升级前先打开自己的 model picker 和 usage 页，看真正打断你的是什么。

Why I Pay $70 for Three AI Coding Agents Instead of $100 for One
Three subscriptions give me separate capacity, different strengths, and a clean way to hand work from one agent to the next.

I asked three frontier AI models to compare the coding-agent subscriptions I use.
Each wrote an analysis. Then I had them cross-check one another. After several rounds and six documents, all three confidently repeated the same load-bearing claim:
To use Claude Opus, I would have to replace my $20 Claude Pro subscription with the $100 Max plan.
They were all wrong.
Opus 5 was already available in my $20 account. I found it by opening the model picker.
The models had relied on one another. None had checked the source. They kept refining the details while the premise beneath the entire comparison remained false.
That was a useful reminder. Agreement among models can reflect shared inputs and shared blind spots.

All three systems failed together, so their mistake could not make the case for a three-subscription setup. It taught me to verify anything tied to a live account, current pricing, or product availability. When the question concerns what your account includes today, open the account.

The same experience also clarified why I keep all three subscriptions. Each gives me another pool of capacity, another approach to a problem, and another way to keep the work moving.

What $70 buys me
My current monthly stack is:
Claude Pro with Claude Code: $20
ChatGPT Plus with Codex: $20
SuperGrok with Grok Build: $30
That is $70 across three vendors.

This comparison focuses on how capacity is distributed. A premium plan can provide substantially more usage inside one ecosystem. That may be the right purchase for someone whose work depends heavily on that ecosystem.

I spread my spending across three vendors because I value options.
Each subscription has its own usage pool. When one reaches a short-term or weekly limit, the others remain available. If one agent keeps repeating an unproductive approach, I can hand the repository to a fresh system with a different set of assumptions.

One provider gives me a deeper pool of capacity. Three providers give me several pools and more places to move the work.

The roles I currently assign
I avoid turning these assignments into a universal ranking. The models change, the products change, and different repositories reward different strengths.
Here is how I currently route the work.
Claude is my hard-problem specialist. I use it for architecture decisions, difficult debugging, cross-cutting changes, and bounded problems where judgment matters more than task length.
Codex is my endurance agent. It gets longer repository campaigns, migrations, unfamiliar codebases, repeated test-and-fix loops, and work that may need to continue until the suite is green.
Grok Build is my greenfield and UI builder. I use it for prototypes, product exploration, interface work, and quickly turning an idea into a first working version. It also gives me a third pool of coding capacity when the others are unavailable.

The handoff is the system
Three subscriptions become useful when the work can move between them cleanly.
Opening three terminals and pasting the same giant prompt into each one wastes capacity and invites competing edits.
When an agent reaches a limit or repeats the same failed approach, I stop at a coherent boundary. I run the relevant tests, commit a checkpoint, and give the next agent a compact brief:
Repository, branch, and commit
Goal and definition of done
Work already completed
Approaches already tried and why they failed
Current test results and exact failures
Files or constraints that must remain unchanged
The single next action
How to prove the task is complete
The “already tried” line is especially important. Without it, the receiving agent may spend its own allowance rediscovering the same dead end.
The next agent starts with the current state and builds its understanding directly from the repository and diff. The old transcript usually stays behind.
I also keep one writer per working tree. If two agents must work simultaneously, they use separate branches or worktrees.

The honest case for one premium plan
Three subscriptions add real overhead.
One larger plan may make more sense when:
One provider is the only service approved for the code or data involved
Your workflow depends deeply on one model or ecosystem
You repeatedly exhaust that provider while barely using the alternatives
Reconstructing context costs more time than the handoff saves
A particular problem genuinely needs more capacity from one specific agent
You value one configuration, one permission model, and one set of habits

Why the portfolio works for me
At this point, the decision comes down to how I want to distribute capacity.
A $100 plan concentrates more capacity inside one vendor. My $70 stack spreads capacity across three agents and gives me cross-vendor handoffs.
For my current workload, the portfolio wins. Coverage is my bottleneck. I need the ability to move a task when one agent reaches a limit or stops making progress.
Before upgrading, open your model picker and usage page. Track what actually interrupts your work.
If one provider carries most of your meaningful tasks, a larger plan with that provider may pay for itself. If work moves successfully among several tools, the diversified stack is earning its keep.
Plan names, prices, included models, and usage limits change. This reflects US monthly pricing and the models available in my accounts on September 1, 2026. Check your own model picker, usage page, current plan terms, data controls, and employer policy before copying the setup.
